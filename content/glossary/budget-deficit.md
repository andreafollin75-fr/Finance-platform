## Definition

A government runs a budget deficit when it spends more in a year than it collects in taxes and other revenue. The gap is covered by borrowing, mostly by issuing government bonds, which adds to public debt. When revenue exceeds spending, there is a **budget surplus**.

Like debt, the deficit is usually expressed as a **percentage of GDP**. EU rules set a reference value of 3% of GDP.

The **primary** deficit is the deficit excluding interest payments on existing debt.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* A government collects €900 billion and spends €1,000 billion in a year. Its deficit is €100 billion. If GDP is €2.5 trillion, that is a deficit of 4% of GDP.

## Why it matters

- Deficits add to public debt and can push up government bond yields.
- During recessions, deficits normally widen, as tax revenue falls and support spending rises. This can help cushion the downturn.
- Reducing deficits ("fiscal consolidation" or "austerity") can be politically difficult and may slow growth in the short term.
