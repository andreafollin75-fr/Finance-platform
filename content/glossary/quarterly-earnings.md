## Definition

Listed companies regularly report their financial results. In the United States, companies file quarterly reports with the SEC; many European companies publish quarterly updates too, while others report in more detail twice a year. The key figures are:
- **revenue** (or sales): money brought in from customers;
- **net income** (profit): what is left after all costs and taxes;
- **earnings per share (EPS)**: net income divided by the number of shares.

**Earnings season** is the few weeks after each quarter ends, when most large companies report.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* Analysts expected a company to earn $1.20 per share for the quarter; it reports $1.35. That is an "earnings beat", and the share price often rises, though the reaction also depends on what management says about the future.

## Why it matters

- Profits are, in the long run, what drives share prices.
- Markets react to results compared with **expectations**, not to the absolute numbers: good results can still disappoint if investors hoped for more.
- Results from big companies give clues about the health of consumers and the economy.
