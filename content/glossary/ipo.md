## Definition

An initial public offering is the process by which a private company becomes **publicly traded**. It sells new shares (and sometimes existing owners sell some of theirs) to investors, and those shares then start trading on a stock exchange.

Investment banks usually help set the **offer price**, gauge demand from investors and organise the sale. In the UK, an IPO is often called a **flotation**.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* A company sells 50 million new shares at $20 each in its IPO, raising $1 billion. On its first day of trading the price rises to $24: the IPO is described as having "popped" 20%.

## Why it matters

- An IPO lets a company raise money to grow, and lets early investors and founders sell part of their stake.
- A busy IPO market is usually a sign of investor confidence; when markets fall, companies tend to delay their listings.
- New listings can be very volatile in their first months of trading.
