## Definition

A stock index summarises the prices of a group of shares in a single number. Its daily change tells you, in one figure, whether that part of the market went up or down. Well-known indices include:
- **S&P 500**: about 500 large US companies, weighted by market capitalisation;
- **Nasdaq Composite**: the shares listed on the Nasdaq exchange, with a heavy weight in technology; the **Nasdaq-100** covers its 100 largest non-financial companies;
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average**: 30 large US companies, weighted by share price rather than size;
- **Euro Stoxx 50**: 50 large companies from euro area countries;
- national indices such as the **FTSE 100** (UK), **DAX** (Germany) and **CAC 40** (France).

## Example

When a news headline says "the S&P 500 fell 1%", it means the combined value of the companies in that index dropped by about 1% that day, with the biggest companies counting the most.

## Why it matters

- Indices are the quickest way to follow the mood of a market.
- They serve as benchmarks: fund managers are judged on whether they beat their reference index.
- Many investors buy index funds or ETFs that simply copy an index, at low cost.
