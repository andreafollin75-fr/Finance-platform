## Definition

The policy rate is the main interest rate controlled by a central bank. It sets the cost at which banks can borrow from, or deposit money with, the central bank, and from there it influences almost every other interest rate in the economy: mortgages, business loans, savings accounts and government bonds.

Each central bank has its own: the **federal funds rate** (target range) for the US Federal Reserve, the **deposit facility rate** as the main signal for the European Central Bank, and **Bank Rate** for the Bank of England.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* If a central bank raises its policy rate by 0.25 percentage points (25 basis points), banks usually pass on part of that increase, so new loans become a bit more expensive and savings accounts pay a bit more.

## Why it matters

- **Raising** the rate ("hiking") makes borrowing more expensive, cools spending and helps bring inflation down.
- **Cutting** the rate makes borrowing cheaper and supports growth and employment, but can push inflation up.
- Markets react not only to the decision itself but to what the central bank says about future decisions, so the press conference after a meeting is often as important as the rate.
