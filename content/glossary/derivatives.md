## Definition

A derivative is a contract between two parties whose value is "derived" from an **underlying** asset or rate. The main types are:
- **futures** and **forwards**: agreements to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date;
- **options**: the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price;
- **swaps**: agreements to exchange cash flows, for example a fixed interest rate for a floating one.

Some are traded on exchanges with standard terms; others are agreed privately "over the counter" (OTC).

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* An airline agrees today to buy fuel in six months at a fixed price through a futures contract. If the market price rises, the airline is protected; if it falls, it misses out on the cheaper price.

## Why it matters

- Derivatives let companies and investors protect themselves against price moves (**hedging**).
- They can also be used to speculate with leverage, which can magnify losses.
- Complex, opaque derivatives linked to US mortgages played an important role in spreading the 2008 financial crisis; rules introduced afterwards pushed much of this market towards central clearing.
