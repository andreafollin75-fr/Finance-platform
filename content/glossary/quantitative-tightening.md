## Definition

Quantitative tightening is when a central bank reduces the size of its balance sheet after a period of quantitative easing. It can do this in two ways:
- **passively**, by not reinvesting the money it receives when the bonds it holds mature (the bonds "run off");
- **actively**, by selling bonds in the market.

Either way, the central bank stops being a big buyer of bonds, which tends to push long-term yields up a little and to withdraw money from the financial system.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* A central bank holds €3 trillion of bonds. Each month €20 billion of them mature, and it decides not to buy new ones with that money. Its holdings shrink by €20 billion a month.

## Why it matters

- QT works in the same direction as interest rate rises: it tightens financial conditions.
- Central banks usually run it slowly and predictably to avoid disrupting markets.
- If QT drains too much money from the banking system, it can cause tensions in short-term funding markets, so central banks watch liquidity carefully.
