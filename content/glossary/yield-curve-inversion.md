## Definition

A yield curve is **inverted** when short-term government bonds pay a higher yield than long-term ones, for example when the 2-year Treasury yields more than the 10-year Treasury. This is the opposite of the normal upward slope.

It usually happens when the central bank has raised short-term rates to fight inflation, while investors expect growth and inflation to weaken in the future, and therefore rates to come down later.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* The 2-year yield is 4.5% and the 10-year yield is 4.0%: the 2s10s spread is −0.5 percentage points, so the curve is inverted.

## Why it matters

- In the United States, an inverted yield curve has come before most recessions of the past several decades, which is why it is watched so closely.
- It is not a perfect predictor: the time between inversion and recession has varied a lot, and an inversion is not always followed by a recession. Treat it as a warning sign, not a forecast.
