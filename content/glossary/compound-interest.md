## Definition

With **simple interest**, you earn interest only on the money you originally put in. With **compound interest**, each period's interest is added to the balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on a larger amount. Interest earns interest.

The formula after *n* years at rate *r* is: **final amount = starting amount × (1 + r)ⁿ**

A handy shortcut, the **rule of 72**: dividing 72 by the annual rate gives roughly the number of years needed to double your money.

## Example

*Hypothetical example.* €1,000 invested at 5% a year:
- with simple interest, after 10 years you have €1,000 + 10 × €50 = €1,500;
- with compound interest, you have €1,000 × 1.05¹⁰ ≈ €1,629.

By the rule of 72, money at 6% a year doubles in about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.

## Why it matters

- Starting to save early has a big effect, because compounding has more time to work.
- It works against borrowers too: unpaid debt, for example on a credit card, can grow quickly.
- Fees and inflation also compound, so small annual differences matter a lot over decades.
